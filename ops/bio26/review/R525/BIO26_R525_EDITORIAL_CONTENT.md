## BIO26-S28-03 | Biodiversity threats and conservation
**Teaching idea:** Biodiversity includes genetic, species and ecosystem diversity. Conservation works best when it interrupts the mechanism causing decline.
**Cause → mechanism → outcome:** Habitat fragmentation reduces connectivity and gene flow, increases edge effects and isolation, and can increase local extinction and genetic risk.
**Compare:** In-situ conservation protects species and ecological relationships in their habitat; ex-situ measures such as seed banks support rescue but do not replace a functioning ecosystem.
**Trap:** A non-native species is not automatically invasive; restoration cannot recreate a species after extinction.
1. A forest is divided into isolated patches. Which action directly addresses isolation? **B.** Build safe ecological corridors connecting patches. Corridors can restore movement and gene flow; adding an invasive species, increasing artificial light or moving every organism to captivity does not repair habitat connectivity.
2. Captive breeding succeeds, but release fails because the original habitat remains polluted and food-poor. **C.** Restore habitat conditions while maintaining the breeding program. More releases do not remove the original limiting pressure.

## BIO26-S10-04 | Vertebrate comparisons
**Teaching idea:** Compare respiration, thermoregulation, reproduction and body covering together; no single feature describes every member of a group.
**Cause → mechanism → outcome:** Different habitats and metabolic demands select for different gas-exchange surfaces, coverings, reproductive strategies and temperature regulation.
**Compare:** Fish generally use gills; amphibians often combine moist skin and lungs; amniotes include reptiles, birds and mammals. Birds and mammals are endothermic but differ in respiratory and integumentary structures.
**Trap:** Monotremes are egg-laying mammals; air sacs ventilate bird lungs, while gas exchange occurs in the lungs.
1. An egg-laying animal has hair and mammary glands. **A.** It is a monotreme mammal. Egg laying alone does not exclude mammalian identity.
2. Which feature supports high metabolic demand during bird flight? **D.** One-way airflow through the lungs with an effective exchange surface. Air sacs assist ventilation; they are not the sole gas-exchange site.

## BIO26-S28-01 | Succession, limiting factors and aquatic systems
**Teaching idea:** Light, water, temperature, salinity, oxygen and nutrients constrain production; disturbance and colonization change community structure.
**Cause → mechanism → outcome:** Nutrient enrichment can trigger algal growth; decomposition of a large dead algal biomass consumes dissolved oxygen.
**Compare:** Primary succession begins without pre-existing soil; secondary succession follows disturbance where soil remains. Photic zones support photosynthesis; aphotic zones lack sufficient light for it.
**Trap:** The photic boundary is not a fixed depth. Some deep communities use chemical energy rather than sunlight.
1. A lake receives excess nutrients, followed by algal growth and lower dissolved oxygen. **B.** Microbial decomposition consumes dissolved oxygen. Nutrients do not convert directly into oxygen.
2. A deep vent community persists without sunlight. **C.** Chemosynthetic organisms use energy from oxidation of inorganic compounds.

## BIO26-S10-01 | Fish classification and adaptations
**Teaching idea:** Gills, fins, lateral-line sensing and osmoregulation solve different problems in aquatic life.
**Cause → mechanism → outcome:** Osmotic gradients drive water and ion movement; gills and kidneys adjust ion uptake and excretion.
**Compare:** Freshwater bony fish tend to gain water and lose ions; marine bony fish tend to lose water and gain salts.
**Trap:** A swim bladder is common in many bony fishes, not universal; the lateral line is sensory, not a propulsive organ.
1. A freshwater bony fish is in water less concentrated than its body fluids. **C.** It produces abundant dilute urine and actively takes up ions. Water enters osmotically.
2. A marine bony fish loses water to the environment. **A.** It drinks seawater and excretes excess salts, including through the gills.

## BIO26-S27-01 | Ecological levels and interactions
**Teaching idea:** The level of organization determines whether the study includes one organism, one species, multiple species or the abiotic environment.
**Cause → mechanism → outcome:** Physical conditions affect organisms, while competition, predation and symbioses affect populations and community structure.
**Compare:** Population = same species in an area; community = populations of different species; ecosystem = community plus abiotic environment. Habitat is where a species lives; niche is how it uses resources and interacts.
**Trap:** Co-occurrence does not prove competition; the species must overlap in a limiting resource. A community alone does not include abiotic factors.
1. Researchers add salinity, temperature and dissolved oxygen measurements to their list of organisms. **D.** They can study interactions between the community and abiotic factors at the ecosystem level.
2. Two bird species compete for limited nest cavities and both show reduced reproductive success. **B.** Competition is a negative/negative interaction.

## BIO26-S09-03 | Echinoderms and invertebrate chordates
**Teaching idea:** Echinoderms and chordates are both deuterostomes, but only chordates have the diagnostic chordate features.
**Cause → mechanism → outcome:** Some invertebrate chordates show notochord, dorsal hollow nerve cord, pharyngeal slits and post-anal tail in the larval stage; some structures regress in adults.
**Compare:** Tunicates may lose several chordate features at adulthood; lancelets retain them. Adult echinoderms often have pentaradial symmetry, while larvae are bilateral.
**Trap:** Notochord is not the same as a vertebral column. Echinoderms are not chordates.
1. A marine larva has a notochord, dorsal hollow nerve cord and post-anal tail; the sessile adult loses some of them. **C.** It remains a chordate, as in tunicates.
2. Which statement is correct? **A.** Echinoderms and chordates are both deuterostomes, but echinoderms are not chordates.

## BIO26-S23-02 | Mitosis and cytokinesis
**Teaching idea:** Mitosis distributes duplicated chromosomes into two nuclei; cytokinesis then divides the cytoplasm.
**Cause → mechanism → outcome:** DNA replicates during S phase; sister chromatids separate during anaphase; nuclei reform in telophase; cytokinesis completes cell division.
**Compare:** Animal cells form a cleavage furrow through an actin-myosin contractile ring; plant cells assemble a cell plate from vesicles.
**Trap:** DNA replication is in S phase, not mitosis. Cytokinesis is distinct from nuclear division.
1. Golgi-derived vesicles collect at the equator of a plant cell after chromosomes reach opposite poles. **B.** A cell plate is forming during cytokinesis.
2. A constriction forms at the middle of an animal cell. **A.** An actin-myosin contractile ring creates the cleavage furrow.

## BIO26-S28-02 | Population density, growth and demography
**Teaching idea:** Population size, density and growth are different measures; births, deaths and migration determine numerical change.
**Cause → mechanism → outcome:** Density is individuals per area or volume; resource limits and demographic rates influence population trajectories.
**Compare:** Exponential growth assumes abundant resources; logistic growth includes carrying capacity K. Density-dependent pressures include competition and disease; drought and storms may act independently of density.
**Trap:** K can change with resources and conditions. Equal population sizes do not imply equal densities when areas differ.
1. A population has 40 births, 12 immigrants, 18 deaths and 4 emigrants. **A.** Net increase = 40 + 12 - 18 - 4 = 30.
2. Two habitats each contain 200 rabbits; one is 10 hectares and the other 20 hectares. **B.** Density in the first habitat is twice the second.
